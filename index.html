Signatura put this page together for the Friends of the Old Toll House — the small, stubborn group who've been patching this roof, chasing thieves off the property, and pouring pancake batter to keep the lights on. This isn't an investment pitch, and nobody's offering shares in a mountain pass. It's simpler than that: a building that matters needs people who'll show up for it. For a business, that can mean putting your name behind something you can point to and say — I helped fix that roof. 01

The landmark

The Old Toll House stands near the foot of Montagu Pass, about two kilometres short of the modern Outeniqua Pass. It went up in the mid-1840s alongside the pass itself — engineered by Henry Fancourt White, opened in the late 1840s — and for decades it was where farmers, transport riders and postal carts stopped to pay their way through the mountain.

The walls are indigenous sandstone, nearly 700mm thick, wrapped around a footprint barely bigger than a large garage — about 16.3 by 6 metres. It doesn't look like much from the road. Stand next to it, with the Outeniquas rising behind, and it's hard not to feel how long it's been standing there. Montagu Pass is often called the oldest unaltered mountain pass still in use in South Africa. This little building is what's left of the people who built it.

c. 1844–1847
Raised in stone alongside the pass, as home to the toll keeper and his family.
1855
Fire takes the original thatch roof. It's rebuilt under corrugated iron and keeps going.
1972
Declared a National Monument, together with the pass it once guarded.
Today
Kept alive by volunteers, after years of neglect, theft, and a second fire that nearly finished the job.
02

Two fires, and it's still standing

This house has burned twice. It's had its yellowwood beams stolen, its doors stripped, its Oregon pine floors carried off piece by piece. Each time, somebody who loved it came back with tools and started again. That's the whole history of this place, really — not grand, just relentless.

03

Why it matters now

George and the Garden Route sell people a story about landscape, craft, and things built to last. The Toll House is that story, in stone — proof of how this region was opened up, taxed, and travelled, long before anyone called it a tourist route. On a good weekend it's a living museum, a coffee stop, a small wedding venue, a reason to pull over on the way up the Pass.

Leave a monument like this without support for long enough and the mountain takes it back — damp in the walls, a door pried open, another storm through a roof nobody could afford to fix. That's not a footnote in a tourism brochure. That's George losing a piece of itself, one closed gate at a time.

04

What the Friends have already done

Friends of the Old Toll House started with one person, Gerda Stols, who decided this building wasn't going to disappear on her watch. Since then, volunteers have fitted security gates, patched roofs, rebuilt interiors by hand, and filled the house with furniture and photographs that local families simply gave away. They've kept the doors open on weekends even when floods closed the Pass and nobody was driving up to see them. What they're holding together, week to week:

  • Keeping the monument weather-tight, secure, and open to the public.
  • Finishing the museum inside, room by room, so the house can keep teaching people its own history.
  • Funding the day-to-day — pancakes, coffee, small events — because that's what actually pays for someone to be here.
  • Building the camping ground and ox-wagon stay they've been planning, as a real, heritage-true source of income.
  • Improving the paths, the gardens, the signage, the Biblical Trail — carefully, without turning the stone into a stage set.
  • Setting aside a reserve, so the next storm, theft, or closed-pass season doesn't shut the doors for good.

07

How you can leave a legacy

A building like this doesn't need one big hero — it needs a long list of ordinary people who decided it mattered to them. Put your name on a bench here and it outlasts you by a century, easily; the stone isn't going anywhere. A small monthly gift adds up more, over the years, than one generous cheque that never comes again. Either way, something with your name on it stays on that mountain long after you're gone.

08

How they say thank you

Nothing plastic gets bolted onto 1840s stone without the Friends agreeing to it first — but there's real, lasting recognition on offer for those who want it:

  • A place on the timber donor board at the house.
  • A mention in what Signatura sends out about this drive.
  • A thank-you on social media or by email, if that's what you'd like.
  • A quiet morning at the house, just for you and whoever you bring.
  • Your name on something new and reversible — a bench, a wagon wheel, a trail post.
If a Section 18A tax certificate matters to you, that depends entirely on the Friends' own NPO and SARS registration — ask them directly before you promise a colleague a deduction. Signatura can't issue a tax receipt for money given to the Friends.

How to get involved

  1. Read this, then go and look properly — oldtollhouse.co.za, or the Facebook page "The Old Toll House Montagu Pass."

  2. If you can, drive the Pass on a Saturday or Sunday — hours have been 09:00 to 15:00, but call ahead — and see the place for yourself.

  3. Work out what you can actually offer: cash, a monthly debit, materials, a day of your trade, or just an introduction to someone who can.

  4. Signatura puts you in touch with the Friends directly — ask for Gerda Stols — so whatever you give goes straight to the people doing the work.

  5. Only ever pay into banking details the Friends confirm with you at the time. If an old flyer has a bank account on it, treat it as out of date until they tell you otherwise.
